JUPITER
11) One Calendar, Many Moons
The Jovian System = R13 (Decade Plus)

Jupiter changes the nature of settlement.

There is no solid Jovian surface on which a human civilisation could spread. Jupiter itself is an enormous hydrogen-and-helium world, more than eleven times Earth's diameter, rotating in less than ten hours beneath bands of cloud and storms. The places humans might actually inhabit are its moons and the artificial habitats built among them.

That makes the Jovian system less like one world and more like an archipelago.

The four Galilean moons alone are radically different. Io is intensely volcanic and deep inside Jupiter's radiation environment. Europa is an ice-covered ocean world. Ganymede is larger than Mercury and possesses its own magnetic field. Callisto lies farther out, heavily cratered and substantially removed from the worst of Jupiter's radiation belts.

They are worlds in their own right, with different environments, local cycles and operational problems.

But they all travel around the Sun together.

For calendar purposes, that shared heliocentric journey defines the Jovian year.

Those local orbital periods matter enormously for transport, illumination, eclipses and operations. They do not define separate civil years.

All four moons share Jupiter's 11.86-Earth-year journey around the Sun.

Year structure 13 complete mears through Janjov–Decjov

In one line:

Jupiter is R13 — thirteen minths per orbital month and thirteen operating mears per Jovian year.

Why R13?

The Jovian orbit lasts about 618.94 standard weeks. A normal operating mear is roughly 48 weeks, so the relationship is:

618.94 ÷ 48 ≈ 12.9

The nearest synchronized structure is therefore R13.

Thirteen mears contain 156 minths. Twelve Jovian orbital months containing thirteen minths each also produce 156 minths. The same orbital year can therefore be read in either direction:

13 mears = 1 Jovian year

and:

13 minths = 1 Jovian orbital month

The nominal R13 structure would contain 624 weeks, slightly longer than Jupiter's physical year, so the calendar removes five or six weeks through the normal expansion joints.

The arithmetic is larger.

The architecture is not.

One calendar across several worlds

This is the genuinely new feature at Jupiter.

Mars needs a civil calendar for one principal inhabited world. The Jovian system may eventually contain communities separated by hundreds of thousands or millions of kilometres, living on moons with dramatically different surface conditions and local astronomical cycles.

Giving each moon its own heliocentric civil year would fragment a system that, from the Sun's point of view, occupies essentially one orbital position. Io's 1.77-day journey around Jupiter is far too short to behave as a human year. Ganymede's seven-day orbit is intriguingly close to a terrestrial week, but coincidence does not make it a useful annual cycle. Callisto's 16.7-day orbit is longer again, yet it remains a local moon-system cycle rather than the annual motion of the civilisation around the Sun.

All four moons share Jupiter's heliocentric position.

So the Jovian orbital calendar belongs to the whole system.

A date such as Jovian Year 18, Sepjov identifies the same broad orbital position whether somebody is living on Europa, Ganymede, Callisto or an artificial habitat between them.

Local moon phase still matters.

It simply answers a different question.

One standard human day

Jupiter also provides the first strong demonstration of why Solarian separates civil time from local rotation.

Jupiter itself turns in less than ten hours, while its major moons orbit the planet on cycles ranging from less than two Earth days to nearly seventeen. Because those moons are tidally locked, their local illumination patterns are tied closely to those orbital periods.

There is no single natural day that makes civil sense across the system.

The Jovian implementation therefore uses the standard Solarian day of 86,400 seconds, divided into the familiar twenty-four hours. Europa can manage its working and lighting patterns around Europa's environment. Ganymede can do the same around Ganymede's. Artificial habitats can create whatever light-dark cycle suits their design.

The shared civil clock does not need to imitate any of them.

For a civilisation distributed across several moons, that is considerably more useful than redefining the day every time somebody changes destination.

A Jovian month is almost an Earth year

Jupiter's orbital period is 11.86 Earth years. One-twelfth of that orbit is therefore about 361 Earth days.

A single Jovian orbital month is almost as long as a terrestrial year.

That is the first point in the book where orbital time begins to feel genuinely alien.

Someone could enter Janjov around the time of one Earth New Year and still be in Janjov as the next terrestrial New Year approached. Yet ordinary human planning does not slow down with Jupiter.

Each Jovian orbital month contains thirteen minths, so staffing cycles, projects, reviews and personal recurrence continue on familiar human timescales while the astronomical calendar moves much more slowly overhead.

The two calendars are now visibly doing different jobs.

Thirteen against twelve

R13 also produces a distinctive internal pattern. A mear contains twelve minths, A through L. A Jovian orbital month contains thirteen.

The minth sequence therefore shifts by one position as each new orbital month begins.

Janjov starts with Minth A and, after passing through the complete A–L sequence, ends with the next A. Febjov begins with B and ends with the next B. The starting letter advances one place with each orbital month until the pattern has walked through the entire minth sequence during the Jovian year.

At the end of Decjov, thirteen complete mears have also elapsed. The year then returns to Janjov and Minth A together.

Residents do not need to manage this as some additional calendar rule. It is simply what happens when thirteen recurring human periods sit inside twelve astronomical ones.

That small pattern is useful because it makes the Repeat Factor visible rather than merely numerical.

The 618- and 619-week year

The nominal R13 structure contains 624 weeks, while Jupiter's physical orbital year lasts about 618.94 weeks. The synchronized civil year therefore uses the two adjacent whole-week forms:

Common Year — 618 weeks

Leap Year — 619 weeks

Because the physical orbit lies very close to 619 weeks, the Leap form dominates the long-run sequence, at approximately:

1 Common Year : 16 Leap Years

In a Common Year, six primary expansion joints contract by one week, producing six 47-week mears among the normal 48-week ones. In a Leap Year, only five such contractions are required.

The difference between the two year forms is therefore a single week at one expansion joint. Nothing else about the Jovian calendar changes. The year still contains thirteen mears, 156 minths and twelve orbital months.

The astronomy changes the realised duration.

It does not change the structure.

Local moon time still matters

One Jovian civil calendar does not make the moons interchangeable.

A settlement on Europa will care intensely about Europa's 3.55-day orbit, eclipse periods and radiation environment. Ganymede operations will care about Ganymede's own seven-day local cycle. Transfers between moons depend on constantly changing geometry around Jupiter.

Those cycles belong in local operational systems and astronomical ephemerides. There is no need to choose between them and the common calendar.

A crew on Ganymede could simultaneously know that the Jovian system is in Mayjov, that the organisation is in a particular human minth and that Ganymede occupies a particular local orbital phase around Jupiter.

All three statements can be useful.

They answer different questions.

That separation is one of Solarian's more important design habits: not every astronomical cycle needs to become a civil calendar unit simply because it exists.

A planetary system rather than a planet

Jupiter is a more substantial test than Mars even though the underlying arithmetic remains straightforward. The Repeat Factor rises from R2 to R13. One orbital month becomes almost as long as a terrestrial year. Thirteen human mears pass during a single Jovian orbit, while the minth sequence walks neatly through a thirteen-against-twelve pattern inside the astronomical months.

But the most important change is geographical rather than mathematical.

The calendar no longer serves one inhabited planetary surface. Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Io and future habitats can all share one Jovian year without pretending that they share the same local environment.

That is the step Jupiter adds to the architecture.

Solarian is no longer merely giving one world two useful clocks.

It is giving several worlds one common astronomical home.

Saturn takes that same idea and stretches it much further. Its orbit lasts almost thirty Earth years.

And, unexpectedly, its calendar fits Solarian almost perfectly.
