CIVILISATION
1) When the Solar System Becomes Geography
The Solar System of People

Human civilisation has spent almost its entire existence in one place.

We have crossed oceans, settled continents, built cities in deserts and at the edge of the Arctic, and connected almost every inhabited part of Earth into one economic and communications system. Yet in astronomical terms we have barely moved: every empire, religion, corporation, government and family in human history has lived on the same planet, travelled around the same Sun on the same orbit and organised life beneath broadly the same twenty-four-hour sky.

That is beginning to change, although not quickly and certainly not according to some of the more excitable timetables occasionally produced by the space industry. Permanent cities on Mars are not going to appear because somebody has put a particularly confident date into a presentation; space remains expensive, dangerous and extremely unforgiving of PowerPoint.

The longer-term direction is nevertheless difficult to ignore. Human beings already live continuously away from Earth's surface, machines operate throughout the Solar System, spacecraft are becoming increasingly capable, and technologies such as autonomous systems, robotics, artificial intelligence, closed-loop life support, nuclear power and eventually better propulsion will continue changing what is physically and economically possible.

None of that guarantees settlement. But take a sufficiently long view — not ten years, but a century, five centuries or a thousand — and the stranger outcome may eventually be for a technologically capable civilisation to remain permanently confined to one planet, or maybe just two.

If civilisation continues, it is reasonable to think that we will spread much further.

From missions to places

The first expansion will probably be modest: scientific stations on the Moon, industrial facilities in cislunar space, small communities on Mars and specialist habitats serving particular economic or strategic purposes. For a long time these places may resemble Antarctica, offshore installations or remote military bases more than independent cities.

Then scale changes the category. A facility containing forty rotating specialists remains fundamentally an outpost; a settlement containing tens of thousands of people, children born there, schools, hospitals, businesses and families with no particular intention of returning to Earth has become something else.

It has become a place.

That transition matters because missions and societies require different infrastructure. A mission can borrow most of its institutional assumptions from home — Earth time, Earth law, Earth accounting periods and Earth cultural rhythms — because its fundamental reference point remains Earth. Permanent communities gradually lose that luxury. They develop local institutions because they have to: law, infrastructure, customs, political arrangements, industries, slang, public holidays and arguments about who is responsible for collecting the rubbish.

Somewhere along the way, the settlement stops behaving like an expedition and starts behaving like human geography.

Mars is the obvious candidate, but it is unlikely to be the only one. Ceres and other large asteroids offer material and water; the moons of Jupiter and Saturn provide radically different settlement environments; more distant systems may eventually offer scientific, economic or strategic reasons for people to remain there permanently.

Some settlements may not be attached to natural worlds at all. Large artificial habitats could eventually become easier places for humans to live than hostile planetary surfaces, and once we can build environments rather than merely endure the ones nature provided, human geography is no longer limited to planets and moons.

A mature Solar System may therefore look less like a collection of planetary colonies and more like an archipelago. Earth would remain enormous and important, but it would no longer be alone.

The unglamorous half of becoming multiplanetary

Most discussion of that future concentrates, understandably, on getting there. Propulsion, radiation, life support, energy, food, gravity, medicine and construction all matter; if those problems are not solved, everything else is academic.

But surviving somewhere is not the same as running a civilisation there. Once settlements become permanent, many of the difficult questions become strikingly ordinary: how property works, who owns infrastructure, how children are educated, how banks operate across planets, which jurisdiction applies to a contract signed on Ceres and fulfilled around Jupiter, and how organisations plan when communication delays are measured in minutes or hours.

Civilisation is largely constructed from systems that cease to look impressive once they work. Electricity disappears into the wall, water comes out of the tap, payments clear, records can be found, roads have names and people turn up for work at roughly the agreed time. That boring reliability is one of civilisation's great achievements. A multi-world society will need to reproduce it under conditions for which almost none of our institutions were originally designed.

Some of those problems will be entirely new; others will be familiar problems operating at inconvenient scales.

One of the quietest will be time.

Civilisation runs on calendars

Calendars look passive because they work so well that we barely notice them. They hang on walls, appear on phones and tell us that Christmas is on a Friday this year, seeming more like a formatting convention than a piece of infrastructure.

That badly understates what they do. Schools have terms, companies have quarters, governments have financial years, employees have working weeks, hospitals have rotas, machines have maintenance cycles, loans mature, subscriptions renew, elections recur, while birthdays, religious observances and public holidays locate individuals and communities inside shared sequences.

Remove the calendar and the clocks would continue perfectly.

Civilisation would become remarkably difficult to organise.

That distinction matters because future settlements will have extraordinarily good clocks. Atomic standards, spacecraft clocks, network time systems and astronomical ephemerides will be able to tell them with immense precision when an event occurred and where major objects in the Solar System were when it happened.

A calendar does a different job. A precision time system can say that a maintenance event occurs at a particular number of seconds after an epoch; a civil calendar lets somebody say that it happens on Monday morning in the third week of the reporting period and have thousands of other people understand where that sits in ordinary life.

One measures time; the other organises it.

Permanent human settlement needs both.

The convenience we have never noticed

Humanity has never had to design civil time for a genuinely multi-world civilisation. We have many calendars on Earth, but all operate inside the same underlying astronomical environment: cultures disagree about eras, months, New Year, religious observances and the names attached to dates, yet everybody still inhabits the same rotating planet travelling around the same Sun.

That common physical background does enormous invisible work. London and Tokyo may occupy different time zones, but a year has the same physical duration in both places, Monday occupies the same recurring position and even very different traditional calendars can be converted against one shared planetary chronology. We have therefore been able to treat calendar coordination largely as a matter of convention.

A multi-world civilisation introduces physics into the negotiation.

Mars does not take the same time to orbit the Sun as Earth. Neither does Ceres. Saturn's moons travel around the Sun on a cycle lasting almost three terrestrial decades, while Neptune takes longer to complete one orbit than a human lifetime; artificial habitats may experience day and night according to lighting schedules chosen by engineers rather than rotation supplied by nature.

Those differences do not make coordination impossible. They do force us to ask a question we have never previously needed to answer:

What exactly should a calendar represent?

Local identity, shared civilisation

The obvious response is simply to keep using the Gregorian calendar everywhere, and there is considerable merit in that idea. A common Earth-derived chronology would make records, contracts, history and interplanetary coordination much easier, and some form of Earth chronology seems likely to remain useful for a very long time.

Permanent settlements, however, will also belong somewhere locally. A person born on Mars will not experience Mars as an exotic destination; it will be home, while someone living on Titan may eventually care where Saturn sits in its long journey around the Sun for much the same broad reason that people on Earth care whether it is January or July.

Local civil time therefore has a cultural dimension as well as an engineering one. A mature settlement should not have to choose between possessing a calendar that says something meaningful about the world it inhabits and remaining intelligible to the rest of humanity.

Distance makes that tension more important.

On Earth, most organisations enjoy effectively instantaneous global communications. London and New York can hold a video call despite inconvenient time zones, and humans complain about a five-hour difference because we are remarkably good at redefining inconvenience.

Interplanetary civilisation cannot negotiate with the speed of light. Mars is always minutes away, communications with the giant-planet systems can take far longer, and as settlement moves outward the Solar System increasingly stops behaving like one real-time conversation. That has organisational consequences. A headquarters on Earth cannot continuously manage a distant settlement as though it were a regional office, because local organisations will often need to act before questions and answers can cross the distance between them.

Paradoxically, greater autonomy can make common operating structures more valuable rather than less. Distributed communities still need to understand one another's dates, planning periods and recurring cycles even when they cannot casually resolve every ambiguity in a live conversation.

Calendars therefore occupy an interesting position. They are local enough to become part of a settlement's identity, but structural enough to affect the operation of the civilisation connecting those settlements.

We will probably improvise first

Human beings are unlikely to wait for a grand theory of interplanetary calendrics before establishing permanent settlements. We will improvise, taking familiar time systems with us because that is what explorers and institutions normally do.

Early settlements will inherit conventions from mission control, employers, governments and whatever software happens to be installed. Mars crews may retain Earth dates while counting sols, lunar communities may use familiar Earth chronology alongside local operational schedules, and different organisations may develop incompatible conventions long before any civil authority decides a standard is needed.

That is not a criticism; infrastructure often evolves exactly this way. Railway gauges, electrical systems, computer protocols and measurement standards tend to become most obviously important shortly after incompatible local choices have made interoperability expensive.

Calendars could follow the same path.

The difference is that this time we can see the problem coming. We already know the Solar System's astronomy, know that human beings require recurring social structure, know that permanent settlements will develop local identities, and know that those communities will still trade, travel, communicate and maintain a shared historical record.

That is enough to ask the question before necessity answers it for us.

The scale of the thought experiment

This book therefore asks the reader to look beyond the first lunar base or first crew on Mars and imagine a mature human presence distributed across the Solar System. Earth remains the largest population centre, Mars contains established cities, large asteroids support permanent communities, Jupiter and Saturn possess networks of settlements on moons and in orbit, more distant systems contain smaller populations, and artificial habitats occupy useful locations between them.

Nobody knows whether such a picture takes two centuries, ten centuries or never arrives in precisely that form. The chronology is not important; the structural question is.

If humanity eventually becomes a civilisation of many worlds, how should those worlds organise civil time? Should every settlement remain on Earth's calendar, should each invent its own, should a year always mean a local orbit even when that orbit lasts longer than a human life, and what needs to remain shared when almost everything else becomes local?

Those questions have never mattered before because humanity has never needed to answer them. It is reasonable to think that eventually we might.

Before we design anything

The temptation at this point is to start drawing a new calendar.

That would be premature.

Calendars are among the oldest pieces of civil technology humans possess, and several thousand years of history contain useful lessons about what they actually do. Civil time has always been a negotiated interface between astronomical reality and human requirements rather than a set of units handed down intact by nature.

Earth has also given us a very particular relationship between day, week, month and year, and familiarity makes that relationship feel much more universal than it is.

Before asking what a Solar-System calendar should look like, we therefore need to understand the calendar we inherited, why it looks the way it does and what previous designers have already learned when they tried to take civil time beyond Earth.

That is where we begin.
