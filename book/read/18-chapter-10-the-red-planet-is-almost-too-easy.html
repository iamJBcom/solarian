MARS
10) The Red Planet Is Almost Too Easy
The Martian System = R2 (Extra Year)

Mars is almost suspiciously accommodating to humans, at least in the one dimension this book cares about most: time.

It is unquestionably alien. Mars is a cold desert with a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide, surface pressure below one per cent of Earth's and gravity only about 38 per cent as strong; outside a protected habitat, a human would have more immediate concerns than the calendar.

Its temporal rhythms, however, are oddly familiar. Mars has an axial tilt of about 25 degrees, close to Earth's, and therefore recognisable seasons. More importantly, its solar day — the sol — lasts about 24 hours 39 minutes and 35 seconds, only forty minutes longer than the terrestrial day.

A Martian settlement could therefore organise mornings, evenings, work and sleep around a natural environmental cycle surprisingly close to the one humans evolved with.

The year is where the resemblance ends.

Mars requires about 687 Earth days, or 668.6 Martian sols, to complete one orbit of the Sun. Its year is therefore almost twice Earth's, while its appreciably eccentric orbit makes the seasons noticeably unequal in length.

That combination — a very usable local day and a substantially longer year — makes Mars an unusually clean first application of Solarian.

Phobos and Deimos matter to the Martian sky and to local operations, but not to the civil year. The astronomical calendar follows Mars itself around the Sun.

In one line:

Mars is R2 — two minths per orbital month and two mears per Martian year.

Why R2?

The physical Martian year lasts about 95.5 Martian weeks. A normal human operating mear is about 48 weeks, so the underlying relationship is immediately visible:

95.5 ÷ 48 ≈ 2

Mars is therefore R2.

One Martian orbital year contains two operating mears and twenty-four minths, while each of the twelve orbital months contains two minths. The first mear runs from Janmar through Junmar. The second runs from Julmar through Decmar.

This is the smallest synchronized step beyond the near-overlap we experience on Earth, which makes Mars a useful place to see the architecture before the numbers become larger.

Mars keeps its sol

Mars is the major exception to Solarian's standard 86,400-second civil day.

There is good reason.

The natural Martian sol is only about forty minutes longer than an Earth day, placing it comfortably within a recognisably human waking and sleeping cycle while retaining direct connection to sunrise, sunset and the surface environment. Imposing a separate standard-duration civil day would therefore solve very little while creating an unnecessary second daily rhythm.

Mars instead uses its natural sol. Seven sols make one Martian week, and the rest of the human operating architecture continues normally above it.

This has one consequence already established in the Solarian Week chapter: a seven-sol Martian week is physically slightly longer than the standard seven-day week used by most Solarian settlements, so the Martian weekday phase gradually drifts relative to the shared standard-day lattice. Mars therefore maps to the Solarian Week Index rather than sharing it directly.

Locally, however, none of that makes the Martian week feel unusual. Monday still follows Sunday.

It simply does so about forty minutes later each day than it would on Earth.

A 95- or 96-week year

The physical Martian year lasts 95.51 weeks, leaving it almost exactly halfway between two adjacent whole-week civil forms. Solarian therefore represents the orbit using a mixture of 96-week Common Years and 95-week Leap Years.

The long-run balance is close to even: approximately 18 Common Years for every 17 Leap Years.

That produces a remarkably simple implementation. In a Common Year, all twenty-four minths retain their normal four-week duration, giving:

24 × 4 weeks = 96 weeks

In a Leap Year, the final Minth L of the second mear contracts from four weeks to three:

23 × 4 weeks + 3 weeks = 95 weeks

That one adjustment is enough.

There is no need for secondary expansion joints, complicated distribution rules or irregular month structures across the rest of the year. Mars uses the primary expansion joint exactly as intended.

The A–L minth sequence therefore repeats twice during one Martian orbit, once in each mear. Almost every orbital month contains eight weeks, almost every minth contains four, and only the final L of a Leap Year changes.

For a world whose physical year contains 668.6 sols, that is a remarkably small amount of civil irregularity.

A good first test

Mars is a satisfying first application because neither side of the architecture has to surrender very much. The planet keeps its natural sol and its real heliocentric year. Human beings keep a seven-position week and a familiar operating rhythm of minths and mears.

The difference between the continuous physical orbit and the discrete civil calendar is absorbed through a single, predictable expansion joint. And the relationship between the human and astronomical scales is as simple as it could reasonably be:

Mars = R2

Mars therefore shows Solarian under unusually favourable conditions.

The next destination does not.

Around Jupiter, there is no inhabited planetary surface at the centre of the calendar at all. Any future civilisation is more likely to be distributed across several major moons and artificial habitats, each with its own local environmental cycles but all travelling around the Sun together. The orbital year also jumps from less than two Earth years to nearly twelve.

That is where Solarian stops looking like a Martian calendar system and starts behaving like a calendar architecture for planetary systems.
